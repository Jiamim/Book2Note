Kai is a nine year-old boy who is doing pretty well in the fourth grade. He's in the middle reading and math groups and has some friends. Overall, he's doing fine. After six weeks of the new school term, the teacher is starting to call or send emails home two or three times a week. Apparently, Kai is disrupting others and getting into trouble for talking during work or study time and not doing what he's supposed to do. He has been sent to detention three times already in a two-week period. The teachers calls include reminder that she has 23 other students to attend to that she cannot do anything special for Kai, and because of that that his mom needs to take care of this problem. Furthermore, the teacher says that if this keeps up, she'll recommend Kai for a special education placement where someone else can deal with this and his disruptive behavior. Kai's mother feels that the pressure is on her but that she's not in a good position to deal with the problem. "I'm not at school," she says. "What can I do to help?" She has told Kai, "You better behave at school or else you'll get into trouble at school and at home." How come saying that to Kai is not an effective prompt? Try to recall what you've learned in the antecedent lessons. First, the mom statement is more of a threat than a constructive aid to getting the behavior. The tone of voice maybe that of a threat. Understandably, the mother is frustrated and her statement is something we can easily understand. Second, the statement does not specify concretely what Kai should do. It may seem obvious but that will not help. It will be better to say very concretely what Kai should do and should not do. Third, as a prompt, it is too distant from the situation and not followed up with praise for engaging in the right behaviors and will have little or no effect on actually changing what Kai does. Statements that begin with, "You had better do this or that", may increase the child's anxiety but they will not develop behaviors we wish. We can adapt our behavior change tools to this situation. What is the first step you would take to develop a program? Take a moment to consider the case and the techniques you have learned so far. First, we have to get the whole picture. What exactly is the behavior? We look at the teacher's list of disruptive behaviors which includes things like, blurting out in class or getting out of his seat and bothering others. The task for developing a behavior change program almost always begins with clarifying exactly what we are trying to increase and decrease. What techniques would you use to modify Kai's behaviors at school? Is there anything you learned in this module that can be applied here? Let us pause to think about this. Because the teacher cannot give Kai the one-on-one attention necessary to carry out a full behavior change program in school, we are going to do a home-based reinforcement program. It has two main parts. The first part is a point chart setup at home. Kai can earn points each day based on his performance in class. The points can be exchanged for things that Kai would like. The mom consults Kai and he lists these various rewards. Staying up a half hour late for bed on a weeknight or an hour more on a weekend. More computer time, cooler sneakers replaces the existing pair, baseball cards, rental of a video, anything that he and the mother agree to might be as rewards. It turns out his mother agrees to everything except getting him a cell phone. Point values are calibrated so that some things can be earned quickly. Two or three points are earned staying up late, for example. Maybe three points for 15 minutes more of computer time, and then some things that he actually has to save up for maybe more time that something will happen on a weekend, baseball whatever. There are also bigger items, smaller items that he uses the various point values for. The second part is a way of measuring behavior at school to determine how many points Kai can earn at home. Each weekday, Kai takes a white three by five inch card to school. On the card are three types of smiley faces, each with a number. Two has a very cheery smiley face and it means that Kai had a really good day with very little or almost no disruptive behavior. One has a gentle smiling face and it means that Kai was a little disruptive and was not really great but not out of control or horrible. Zero has a neutral flat mouse face and means Kai was pretty disruptive. There was a sheet at home and email to the teacher to define what is a two, one, or zero. So, the teacher knows what everyone has in mind. His mother asks the teacher to circle two, one, or zero, on the three by five card, initial it and send it home with Kai each day. The teacher feels that she can easily do that. If for some reason the card does not get scored or the teacher forgets, she emails mom what happened that day. The program is simple, each day Kai earns one point just for bringing the card home and handing it to his mom. In other words, just participating in the program Kai earns something. He earns two more points if the teacher circled a two. One point if the teacher circles a one, and zero points if the teacher circles zero. He loses a point if he start to argue with his mom or the teacher about points earned that day. The way a baseball player can get kicked out of the game for arguing with the ump about a called strike. Here's a question for you: When would be a good time for the mother to review Kai's card for that day? Remember, we want to reduce the distance between the behavior and the consequence. So, as soon as Kai comes home from school, the mom looks at the card and adds point to the chart on the wall and notes his stars or points for the day, and the total that he has in his account. After the points are entered, Kai has the choice of cashing in by identifying something he wants to buy or waiting for some bigger reward later. Other than points, what are some consequences we can use to reinforce Kai's behavior? First, we can ask the teacher to praise Kai anytime he gets a two or a one. We do not have control over that but we can control how Kai is praised when he comes home. When Kai arrives home, his mother praise him even for a one, which should be shaping, and praises a two which much more enthusiasm. Now, let us stop to consider what we would do if Kai receives a zero. The best thing to do if Kai receives zero is to give him a point on the chart for bringing the card home and saying in a calm matter-of-fact tone, "Tomorrow is another chance and you may earn more points then." It is important to keep this brief not to interrogate with questions like, "What did you do to make the teacher circle a zero?" All such comments undermine the program. They are setting events that are inherently confrontational and will promote escape and avoidance as, for example, making it so he never brings the card home. In this particular case, the mother adds something special when Kai receives a two, she calls it a two treat licorice. Because it is given for a two and the two of them together eat some licorice together. She keeps around some licorice for them to have that whenever there is a two on the card. Keep in mind that this is not needed in the program but it is a way of making a two extra special and to speed up shaping a little bit with some extra incentive. Also, besides the licorice as a reinforcement, it is some parent-child time together and that's a good thing to do as well. In general, we try to stay away from using sweets as reinforcers. Many parents are against using food and sweets and I tend to avoid them as rewards. Yet this was fine idea and a special time for the mother and child to do something together for special days. From what you remember about antecedes what are some prompts Kai's mother can give in the morning before school? Take a moment to write an example down. When the mother drops off Kai at school, she provides a gentle reminder as she hands Kai the card. She says, "Good luck today in earning points. I'm thinking about you and I hope you can do it. It is hard to do and don't feel too bad if you cannot get all the points today." This is more of a setting event than a prompt, and it takes some of the pressure off which increases the likelihood that he will do what is asked of him. She can also consider being more specific about the disruptive behavior such as saying, "Good luck today and please remember to pay attention to your teacher and not to blurt out answers to questions." Even better than good luck, it would be good to give a challenge and not to demand perfection as part of that. She could say, "I bet you cannot sit really well without interrupting the class for now until lunchtime." In a home-based reward program, how do you think we can use shaping? In other words, how can we build on more complex behaviors? Let us take a moment to consider that. Think about upping the ante for consistency and consecutive days of getting a two. After the first week, Kai is getting ones and twos. The mother adds one extra bonus point each time Kai has two twos in a row. So, if on Monday and Tuesday he gets a two, he gets the usual points, but for the two in a row, he gets a special bonus point. Then, on Wednesday he has a two again. He gets the points for Wednesday but because he has Tuesday and Wednesday, he gets a bonus point for two in a row. That practice of adding a special incentive for consistency could be stretched out to three or four and so on, but proceed slowly to be sure the early steps are stable. In summary, asking your teacher or other supervising adult to fill out a card is reasonable and effective. In some recent cases, we have omitted the card and had teachers or others text the parent at the end of the day. But we prefer the physical card because it is tangible feedback that the child can touch and see. Please remember that this sort of home-based reinforcement program is useful for school behavior problems. You could also use this kind of problem for athletic practices, day camp, and other such situations where we cannot ask the adult in-charge to stick to a special program like administering praise for positive opposites on any regular basis that would have a significant effect.
